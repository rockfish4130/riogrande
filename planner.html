How likely is a runnable week, Colorado Canyon through Santa Elena Canyon? It's a statistical model trained on every season since 2007 at USGS 08374550, Rio Grande near Castolon. Everything is computed in your browser from the same data the flow chart uses. The full method, assumptions and validation are further down this page.

How the model works

1. What counts as a runnable trip

A trip that starts on day t is runnable if the USGS daily mean discharge at Castolon stays between your min and max on every day from t to t+L−1, where L is the trip length (default 7). One day outside the range fails the trip. That's deliberately strict: a single 5,000 cfs flash flood, or one day at 150 cfs, changes the trip. Because these are daily means, a short spike inside a day can be missed, and the daily max is shown on the flow chart if you want to check.

The thresholds come from Southwest Paddler's guides for Colorado Canyon and Santa Elena Canyon. Both give minimum 200 cfs, optimum 300–1,000, maximum 1,200.

2. Which gauge (and why it's an assumption)

The guides don't name a gauge. Their links point to "USGS Gauge / NOAA River Gauges" pages that no longer resolve. This planner assumes USGS 08374550, Rio Grande near Castolon, for three reasons: it's the only gauge on this stretch with a long, continuous public daily record (continuous since August 2007) and an API, it sits at the Santa Elena take-out, and the guides give identical numbers for both canyons, which suggests one reference gauge.

Caveats. Castolon is below the Terlingua Creek confluence, so a storm on Terlingua Creek shows up here but not in Colorado Canyon or most of Santa Elena. During dry baseflow periods, which are exactly the conditions that decide fall trips, Terlingua Creek adds roughly nothing, and Castolon should track flow through both canyons. Gains and losses between Presidio, Lajitas and Castolon (seepage, evaporation, irrigation diversions) aren't modeled. For the upstream Colorado Canyon section, the IBWC gauge below the Rio Conchos near Presidio (08374200) would be the better reference, but its data isn't available through the USGS API.

3. Data

  • USGS daily values, parameter 00060 (discharge, cfs), statistic 00003 (daily mean). The flow chart's daily GitHub Action pulls them into data/YYYY.json. Recent values are provisional and may be revised.
  • Training uses every year with data (partial 2007 through the latest complete day). When you forecast "as of" a date, that year is always held out, so past forecasts shown here are true out-of-sample hindcasts.

4. The model

For each possible start date, the question is "will this trip be runnable?", answered with a probability. It's a logistic regression, refit separately for every lead time k (days from the as-of date to the trip start):

P(runnable | x) = 1 / (1 + e−(β₀ + β₁x + β₂x²)),  x = log₁₀( min daily mean over the 7 days ending on the as-of date )

  • Predictor. The lowest daily mean in the last week is a proxy for baseflow: the water that's actually still in the river, mostly from the Rio Conchos. Storm spikes pass in a few days and mostly don't persist, so using the minimum ignores them. It's log-transformed because flows here range from 0 to about 50,000 cfs. Zeros are floored at 1 cfs.
  • Shape. The quadratic term lets the chance fall off on both sides, so "the river's nearly dry" and "the river's in flood" can both mean low odds. It carries full weight up to a 30-day lead. The quadratic version overfit at long leads in cross-validation, so from 30 to 60 days its prediction is blended linearly into a model that's linear in x, and beyond 60 days only the linear model is used. The blend avoids a step in the curve where the two models meet.
  • Training sample. For as-of date d and lead k, every training year contributes pairs (x on day d′, outcome of the trip starting on d′+k) for d′ within ±10 days of the same calendar date, sampled every 2 days. That's about 11 pairs per year and roughly 200 per fit. Neighboring pairs overlap and are strongly correlated, so the effective sample size is closer to the number of years (about 18) than to 200. Keep that in mind when reading any single number.
  • Regularization. A ridge penalty (λ = 0.1) on the slope terms, fit by Newton–Raphson. Probabilities are capped at 2–90%. Cross-validation showed the uncapped model was overconfident at the top end: when it said 90% or more, the trip worked only about 78% of the time. Eighteen seasons can't support near-certainty.
  • Base rate. The dashed line is the share of runnable outcomes in that same training sample. It's what you'd guess from the calendar alone, and it's the benchmark the model has to beat.

5. How the design was chosen

Three predictors were compared by leave-one-year-out Brier skill: the median of the last 7 days, the median of the last 30 days, and the minimum of the last 7 days. The 7-day minimum was slightly best at every lead time. Adding the quadratic term helped at leads up to about a month (skill roughly +0.32 → +0.36 at one week) and hurt beyond about two months, which is where the blended design in section 4 comes from. If today's baseflow is outside the range seen on this date in the training years, the page says so, because the model is then extrapolating. With one predictor and about 18 seasons, more inputs would mostly fit noise.

6. What's not in the model (yet)

  • Rio Conchos reservoir storage (La Boquilla, Luis L. León, and others). Conchos water is what sustains fall flow, so this is probably the single most useful missing input at 2–4 month leads. The storage records are published by CONAGUA in Mexico, and there isn't yet a clean feed for them.
  • ENSO (El Niño / La Niña). It's easy to get, but with about 18 seasons, one more predictor is as likely to add noise as signal. It will be tested before it's added.
  • Weather forecasts and storms. The model knows nothing about rain in the next few days. Flash floods (Sep 25, 2026 peaked at 15,200 cfs; Nov 4, 2016 at 14,600) are the main reason even a good-looking week can fail. Check the NWS Presidio forecast in the final week before you go.
  • Human decisions. Conchos releases are managed, partly around Mexico's delivery obligations under the 1944 US–Mexico Water Treaty. A release decision can change the picture in days, and no model here can see it coming.

7. How to read it

  • Inside about 2 weeks: the model is meaningfully better than the calendar, because the river doesn't change much in two weeks without a storm.
  • 1–2 months out: it has modest skill. It separates "the river is basically dry right now" years from "there's real baseflow" years, but it can't place a week precisely.
  • 3+ months out: it has essentially no skill beyond the calendar, and the chart fades that range to say so. Use the "usually good weeks" view for long-range planning.
  • If you can be flexible, flexibility helps more than precision. The monthly table shows how often some start date in the month worked.
